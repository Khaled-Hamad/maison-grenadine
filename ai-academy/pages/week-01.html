WEEK 01 / TEACHING DRAFT

AI literacy and responsible first use

Recognise useful assistance, unsupported claims and the decisions that remain yours.

Download the complete kit ↓

Status: teaching draft, not learner-tested. Part of the full 24-week edition. Audience: adults with basic browser and document skills; no AI experience. Time: 6 hours, including independent work. Materials: this pack, a document editor or paper, and optionally an approved Claude or Gemini account. No paid plan, programming, or Claude Code is required. Instructor companion: Sample lesson pilot guide.

1. What you will be able to do

By the end, you can:

  1. Explain AI, machine learning, and generative AI in your own words.
  2. Distinguish a model, an application, a subscription, and an API in a concrete example.
  3. Decide whether three professional tasks need ordinary software, AI assistance, or human authority.
  4. Compare a manual output with an AI-assisted output using the same factual checklist.
  5. Identify information you must not upload without authorization, unsupported claims, and a task you should not delegate.

Your submission: two versions of an invitation, a comparison checklist, a short risk log, and five exit-question answers. Quality and judgment matter, not which tool you use.

3. Start with a prediction

Before reading further, write one sentence for each:

  • Something you think AI is good at.
  • Something you would not trust it to do.
  • A fact you would check even if its answer sounded confident.

There are no marks for prior knowledge. Keep these notes to compare with your final answers.

4. Learner notes: the essential ideas

AI is a broad category, not one chatbot

Artificial intelligence is a broad term for computer systems that perform tasks such as recognizing speech, identifying patterns, generating media, or recommending options.

Machine learning is a major approach within AI: a system learns patterns from examples rather than having every decision rule written individually. Deep learning is a family of machine-learning methods using layered neural networks. You do not need to understand the mathematics this week.

Generative AI produces new text, images, audio, video, or other content based on learned patterns and the information provided to it. A prediction system might estimate tomorrow's demand; a generative system might draft an explanation of a demand forecast. Modern products can combine both.

Do not classify a product from its appearance alone. A chat box might use a model, fixed rules, search, or several methods together.

Automation is not automatically AI

A spreadsheet adding a column follows a defined calculation. A rule forwarding messages containing an exact word is ordinary automation. Neither needs generative AI.

If a deterministic rule solves the problem reliably, it may be cheaper and easier to check than a model. AI is not an upgrade for every task.

Four things people often confuse

Term Plain-language meaning Example
Model The trained system that produces predictions or generated responses A language model selected inside an assistant
Application The software interface and features through which you use models and other tools The Claude or Gemini chat application
Subscription An account's commercial access arrangement, with particular features and limits Claude Pro; exact current benefits must be checked
API A defined way for software to request another service's capabilities A website sending a request to a model service

Paying for a chat subscription does not automatically pay for your website's API calls or hosting. Claude Code is a development tool, not a requirement for today's exercise. Its plan access is covered later.

Fluent does not mean true

An assistant can produce confident wording that is inaccurate or unsupported. This is often called a hallucination. It can invent a room, a citation, a price, or an explanation that looks plausible.

It may also omit important details, misunderstand an instruction, or repeat an error in the information you supplied. These are different failure modes; all can matter.

A generated response is a draft to evaluate, not evidence that its claims are correct. Asking "Are you sure?" can help reveal issues, but it does not verify a fact. Check the original source or an appropriate expert.

Responsibility stays with people

The person or organization using an output remains responsible for its use. A model does not become an authorized doctor, engineer, employer, or rights holder because a prompt assigns it that role.

For this course, use public information you are permitted to share or information invented specifically for practice. Do not upload patient records, client contracts, confidential designs, passwords, private messages, or identifiable employee information without the required authorization and approved environment.

Removing a name does not necessarily make a record anonymous. Dates, locations, rare conditions, and combinations of details can identify a person. If unsure, stop and ask the responsible data owner; do not experiment with the real record.

A first-use safety routine

Before sharing: Do I have permission? Does the task need this information?

Before trusting: What claims can I check, and against what source?

Before acting: Who approves this, and what happens if it is wrong?

These are human checks, not guarantees that a system is safe.

7. Independent professional adaptation

Choose one fictional task. Write a three-sentence plan naming the input, intended output, and human reviewer. Then list three questions you must answer before using real information.

Profession Safe fictional task Boundary to address
Engineering Summarize public equipment descriptions Not approval of a safety-critical design
Healthcare Draft general workshop publicity about reading health information No patient-specific advice or real patient records
Social media Draft captions from an invented business brief No invented testimonials or unsupported claims
Art/design Describe three directions for an invented exhibition poster Check permissions for any real reference material

This is a plan, not a second production assignment. No specialist knowledge is required.

Risk log template

Complete at least three rows, including a privacy risk, a factual risk, and an authority/approval risk.

What could go wrong? Who could be affected? Prevention/check Who approves or resolves it?

8. Exit questions

Answer without copying the definitions:

  1. How is a spreadsheet sum different from an AI-generated explanation?
  2. Give an example distinguishing a model, an application, a subscription, and an API.
  3. An answer says the venue is accessible. The source says nothing about accessibility. What do you do?
  4. A colleague removes a patient's name before pasting the record into a personal assistant. Is that enough to proceed? Why?
  5. Name one task you would not delegate to an assistant and one smaller part it might help with safely.

10. Misconceptions and glossary

  • "AI knows everything": it can lack information and produce unsupported answers.
  • "Paid means private enough for any data": approval depends on the environment, contract, purpose, and policies.
  • "More confident means more accurate": confidence of wording is not an evidence check.
  • "No error in one trial means reliable": one example tells you little about other inputs.

Prompt: instructions or input given to a model-based assistant. Output: the response produced. Baseline: the comparison method or result. Verification: checking a claim against appropriate evidence. Sensitive information: information requiring protection because of privacy, confidentiality, security, or other obligations.

Optional depth, within independent-study time: draw AI as a broad category containing machine learning and deep learning. Mark generative AI as a capability category that overlaps modern deep learning, not as a synonym for all AI.

Instructor answer guidance

Instructor companion and answer guidance

Keep this section for feedback after the independent attempt. The separate instructor/pilot guide includes delivery preparation and assessment calibration.

Expected worked result: The draft preserves the price but invents a venue and qualification and reverses the laptop instruction. Correct those details, keep the venue unconfirmed and ask the organiser before publication.

1. Does fluent wording establish truth?

No. Verify consequential claims against evidence.

2. Does removing a patient's name authorise an upload?

No. Indirect identifiers, permission and the approved environment still matter.

3. When is an ordinary spreadsheet preferable?

For a defined calculation with known inputs and checkable formulas.

Facilitation: ask the learner to point to a source, calculation, state transition or artifact supporting their conclusion. Accept defensible alternatives with explicit assumptions. Do not reward confident wording without evidence.

Remediation: identify the failed criterion, explain its consequence, provide a new small input, and reassess the relevant behaviour. Record what was actually observed; do not issue professional approval from a course score.
